Dear Editor and Reviewers,

We sincerely thank the reviewers for their careful reading and constructive comments. The comments helped us identify two central issues in the previous manuscript: the upper level was described too strongly as timetable optimization even though it mainly changed a target headway, and the evaluation did not sufficiently isolate the effect of dynamic timetable decisions or report passenger delay trade-offs. We have substantially revised the manuscript, implementation, experiments, and related-work coverage accordingly. In the revised version, the upper level now directly writes rolling planned dispatch times, while the lower level executes the resulting dynamic timetable through station-level holding. We also added new ablations, classical holding baselines, RL/MARL-style proxy baselines, passenger-delay metrics, timetable-feasibility metrics, and a more conservative discussion of joint training and limitations.

Below we respond point by point.

Reviewer 1, Comment 1

The reviewer notes that the previous method did not directly optimize dispatch times, vehicle schedules, or a timetable, because scheduled times remained unchanged and the upper level only adjusted the target headway used by the lower controller. The reviewer suggests framing the contribution more narrowly as adaptive target-headway tuning.

Response:

We agree with this criticism. In the previous version, the main method was indeed closer to adaptive target-headway tuning than to direct timetable optimization. We have therefore made a substantive methodological revision rather than only changing the wording.

In the revised TransitDuet, the upper policy directly selects the planned headway for the next same-direction dispatch. The simulator converts this upper-level decision into a planned launch time, and that planned launch time is used by the dispatch logic. The baseline timetable is retained only as a reporting reference for planned shift and timetable deviation; it no longer constrains dispatch once the dynamic timetable policy is active. The lower policy then tracks the trip-specific planned headway through station holding.

We revised the abstract, introduction, problem formulation, method section, discussion, and conclusion to make this distinction explicit. We also retained the previous target-headway-only configuration as an ablation rather than presenting it as the main method. In the revised experiments, the rolling-timetable policy is the main TransitDuet configuration, while the old target-headway-only variant is reported separately.

Reviewer 1, Comment 2

The reviewer states that the value of dynamic target headways is not isolated and asks for a strict ablation with a fixed target headway using the same lower SAC controller, as well as the variance of the target-headway sequence and its relationship to realized headway CV.

Response:

We agree, and we added the requested isolation experiments and diagnostics.

First, the revised experiments include the previous target-headway-only variant as a direct ablation. This tests the old mechanism in which the upper changes only the lower controller's reference headway without writing planned launch times.

Second, we added fixed-timetable SAC baselines using the same lower SAC controller. These include a 360-second fixed timetable and a best fixed-timetable setting selected from fixed headways of 300, 330, 360, 390, and 420 seconds. This isolates whether dynamic planned dispatch times add value beyond a strong holding-only SAC controller.

Third, we added timetable-variability diagnostics. The revised manuscript now reports planned-shift variability, dispatch lateness, and the relationship between planned timetable variability and realized dispatch-headway CV. Across ten seeds and 300 final-episode samples for the final rolling-timetable policy, the planned-headway variance and realized dispatch-headway CV show a strong positive relationship, with Pearson correlation 0.994 and Spearman correlation 0.981. We use this result cautiously: it does not mean that more timetable variation is automatically better, but it shows that the planned timetable sequence is not a cosmetic signal and is reflected in realized service regularity.

The revised main comparison shows that the rolling-timetable policy achieves a composite cost of 1.634, compared with 1.687 for the target-headway-only variant and 1.846 for the best fixed-timetable SAC baseline. We therefore now isolate the contribution of dynamic timetable decisions rather than attributing all gains to adaptive target tracking.

Reviewer 1, Comment 3

The reviewer notes that the evaluation omits holding cost and onboard delay, and that a method could reduce out-of-vehicle waiting time and headway CV by holding buses longer while increasing onboard passenger delay. The reviewer asks that the paper at least report average holding time.

Response:

We agree, and we have expanded the evaluation metrics accordingly.

The revised manuscript now reports average holding time, onboard passenger time, and total passenger time in addition to waiting time, headway CV, fleet overshoot, and the composite objective. These metrics are included in the main comparison table and are discussed explicitly in the experiments and discussion sections.

This addition changes the interpretation of the results. For example, the classical Daganzo-style and Xuan-style holding proxies apply much longer average holding, 45.15 seconds and 48.33 seconds respectively, and yield higher total passenger time than the revised TransitDuet policy. By contrast, the final rolling-timetable policy has average holding of 15.59 seconds and total passenger time of 18.15 minutes. The best fixed-timetable SAC baseline has lower headway CV but uses more holding, 25.69 seconds, and has higher total passenger time, 19.92 minutes. We therefore no longer evaluate the method only through out-of-vehicle waiting and regularity; we explicitly report the wait-delay trade-off.

Reviewer 1, Comment 4

The reviewer states that the comparison is incomplete and should include simple classical baselines and recent RL benchmarks, including Daganzo 2009, Xuan et al. 2011, and recent RL/MARL holding studies.

Response:

We agree and have broadened the comparison substantially.

The revised experiments include Daganzo-style holding and Xuan-style two-headway holding baselines as classical analytical references. We also added holding-only SAC fixed-timetable baselines, including a best fixed-timetable SAC baseline, to test whether a strong lower controller alone can explain the gains.

For recent RL/MARL comparisons, we added a same-simulator HAAR-style RL coupling proxy motivated by recent bus holding and MARL studies. We are careful not to claim that this proxy is an exact reproduction of each original paper, because those studies use different networks, simulators, demand assumptions, and control actions. The manuscript now states this explicitly. The goal of the proxy is to test representative same-simulator RL coupling mechanisms under our environment and metrics, not to supersede every published implementation.

In the revised main comparison, the final rolling-timetable TransitDuet policy achieves a composite cost of 1.634. The HAAR-style RL proxy has composite cost 1.802, the best fixed-timetable SAC baseline has 1.846, Daganzo-style holding has 2.040, and Xuan-style holding has 2.025. These additional baselines address the reviewer's concern that the original comparison was too narrow and mostly non-RL.

Reviewer 1, Comment 5

The reviewer states that the bus holding review is limited, that the manuscript discusses timetable optimization too extensively relative to holding and bunching control, and that it misses several recent works including Wang and Sun 2021 and 2023, He et al. 2022, Yu et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2025, and Li et al. 2026.

Response:

We agree and revised the related-work section accordingly.

The revised related-work section now begins with reinforcement learning for bus holding control and explicitly discusses analytical holding baselines, value-based RL, asynchronous MARL, distributional MARL, multi-objective MARL, hierarchical MARL, graph-aware deep RL, systematic RL configuration evaluation, and language-model-assisted reward generation for bus holding. We added and verified the suggested references, including Wang and Sun 2021, Wang and Sun 2023, He et al. 2022, Yu et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2025, and Li et al. 2026. We also added related works on cooperative bus holding and stop-skipping and on robust bus fleet control where relevant.

We also revised the positioning. The manuscript now explains that TransitDuet is not simply an offline timetable optimization method and not simply a holding controller. It addresses the interface between rolling timetable decisions and execution-level holding. This clearer positioning is reflected in the introduction, related work, experiments, and discussion.

Reviewer 2, Major Comment 1

The reviewer asks for a more detailed discussion of current HRL limitations and of how TransitDuet differs from standard option-based or goal-conditioned HRL. The reviewer notes that the main distinction may not be only a common clock or synchronous execution, but the concurrent execution of multiple goals. The reviewer asks why a goal-conditioned options formulation with a shared lower policy conditioned on a bus's active option would be insufficient.

Response:

We thank the reviewer for this helpful clarification. We agree that the issue is not merely the lack of a common clock. The more important distinction is that multiple timetable goals are active concurrently and interact through shared fleet availability, station-level holding, and dispatch lateness.

We revised the introduction, problem formulation, method section, and discussion to make this point explicit. A goal-conditioned lower controller can represent one part of TransitDuet: a dispatched bus carries a planned headway, and the lower holding policy is conditioned on that planned headway. However, this interpretation does not capture the rolling timetable generator. In the revised method, the upper action writes the planned launch time of a future trip, sometimes before the corresponding vehicle is available, while buses dispatched under earlier planned headways continue to operate. Several planned headways are therefore active at the same time, and their effects overlap through fleet constraints and holding decisions.

We now state that a conventional goal-conditioned options formulation can describe the lower tracking controller, but it does not by itself specify how future dispatch times are generated, how timetable feasibility is audited, or how passenger wait, onboard delay, fleet overshoot, and dispatch lateness are credited back to asynchronous dispatch-time decisions. This is why the revised framework uses a dynamic timetable channel, holding-feedback state, and per-dispatch hindsight credit rather than presenting TransitDuet as a direct application of standard options.

Reviewer 2, Major Comment 2

The reviewer questions whether the paper truly demonstrates that target-headway planning and holding control must be optimized jointly. The reviewer suggests an ablation comparing independently trained upper and lower models assembled after training with the jointly trained TransitDuet model.

Response:

We agree with this concern and have added the requested independent-assembly ablation.

In the new ablation, the lower controller is first trained under a fixed timetable, then frozen and paired with a trained timetable upper. This tests whether TransitDuet's gains require joint adaptation or whether an independently trained tracking controller is sufficient. The jointly trained rolling-timetable policy achieves composite cost 1.634, whereas the independent-assembly variant has composite cost 1.929. This gap supports the value of joint adaptation in our setting.

At the same time, we have moderated the claim. The revised manuscript no longer argues that joint training is universally necessary. Instead, it describes TransitDuet as a jointly trainable framework and reports the independent-assembly result as empirical evidence that, in this corridor and simulator, adapting the lower controller to the rolling timetable improves performance. We also added a discussion paragraph noting that if a fixed-timetable lower controller can be paired with a timetable upper without loss in other settings, then the appropriate claim is complementarity between adaptive planning and execution-level control rather than a universal necessity of joint training.

Reviewer 2, Minor Comment 1

The reviewer states that Figure 6 was interpreted as showing stabilization of lambda, but the figure did not show a clear plateau; moreover, lambda convergence would not demonstrate constraint satisfaction. The reviewer recommends moderating the interpretation and supplementing it with actual constraint cost.

Response:

We agree. We have removed the over-strong interpretation that the lambda trajectory alone demonstrates convergence or constraint satisfaction. The revised text treats the Lagrangian multiplier as an adaptive penalty signal rather than direct evidence of feasibility.

We also shifted the evidence for constraint behavior to directly measured operational quantities. The revised experiments report fleet overshoot, headway CV, holding time, onboard passenger time, total passenger time, and dispatch lateness in the main tables. The method and related-work sections now state explicitly that the fleet and headway constraints are soft constraints and that the adaptive penalty mechanism does not provide a worst-case feasibility guarantee. In other words, constraint behavior is now evaluated through the actual measured costs and service metrics rather than inferred from the multiplier trajectory.

Reviewer 2, Minor Comment 2

The reviewer notes an inconsistency in the lower-level action-space description. The action was first described as a non-negative holding duration in [0, 60], but later text used a symmetric range with negative actions, implying release actions or reduced inter-station idle. The reviewer asks for clarification because the experimental setup has no such inter-station idle component and therefore should have zero as the lower bound.

Response:

We agree and have corrected this inconsistency.

The final experimental configuration uses a non-negative discrete holding action. The lower policy chooses from the operational holding grid 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 20, and 30 seconds. There are no negative holding or release actions in the revised main method. We removed the misleading symmetric action-space description from the main formulation and clarified in the problem formulation, method section, appendix, and hyperparameter table that the lower action is a non-negative holding duration. Any discussion of negative actions or inter-station idle has been removed from the main experimental setup.

Reviewer 2, Minor Comment 3

The reviewer states that it was not clear what belongs in Section 3 versus Section 4, because several design choices specific to TransitDuet appeared in the problem formulation, making it difficult to distinguish problem-specific assumptions from methodology design choices.

Response:

We agree and have reorganized the presentation.

The revised Section 3 is now explicitly framed as the problem formulation. It defines the transit system, the two coupled decision processes, the state and action domains, the soft constraints, and the timescale separation. It also includes a short relation to goal-conditioned options because that point is part of defining what the problem formulation does and does not capture.

The revised Section 4 is now the TransitDuet framework. It introduces the architecture, upper timetable policy, lower holding policy, dynamic timetable coupling, holding-feedback channel, hindsight credit, adaptive fleet-overshoot penalty, training schedule, auxiliary mechanisms, and implementation details. We added an explicit opening paragraph in Section 3 stating that learning architecture and training mechanisms are introduced separately in Section 4. This should make the boundary between problem assumptions and algorithmic design clearer.

We again thank the reviewers for the detailed and constructive feedback. The revision changes both the method and the evidence base: the upper level now directly optimizes rolling planned dispatch times, the previous target-headway-only version is treated as an ablation, and the evaluation now includes delay-aware metrics, classical holding rules, RL/MARL-style comparisons, fixed-timetable SAC baselines, independent assembly, and timetable-variability diagnostics.
