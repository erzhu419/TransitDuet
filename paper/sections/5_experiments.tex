\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate TransitDuet on an event-driven bus microsimulation. In the main configuration, the upper policy selects rolling planned-headway adjustments that the simulator converts into planned dispatch times. The lower holding controller tracks the resulting dynamic timetable. A target-headway-only configuration is retained as an ablation.

%% ============================================================
\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Simulation environment.}
The simulator models a single bidirectional bus corridor with 22 stations (11 per direction) and 264 trips per service day. Passenger arrivals follow time-varying origin--destination demand matrices with stochastic per-hour demand multipliers $\mathcal{N}(1,0.15^2)$ clipped to $[0.3,2.0]$. Link travel times are log-normal with route stochasticity $\sigma_{\mathrm{route}}=1.5$ unless otherwise stated. The simulation step is one second; passenger states update every 20 seconds. The upper policy is event-driven and acts after each actual dispatch to select the planned-headway adjustment for the next same-direction trip. The lower policy acts at station arrivals.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
The main method uses a categorical upper policy over five planned-headway adjustments from $-60$ to $+60$\,s and a categorical lower holding policy over eight holding bins from 0 to 30\,s. We compare it with the following ablations and baselines:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Target-headway-only variant: the upper changes the lower reference headway but does not alter planned launch times.
    \item Wider-action rolling timetable: an ablation with a nine-bin upper action and 60\,s maximum holding.
    \item Continuous rolling timetable: timetable mode with a continuous upper action instead of the categorical timetable action.
    \item No-context rolling timetable: timetable mode without the lower state additions for last action, neighboring planned gaps, and station phase.
    \item Fixed-timetable SAC (holding-only SAC): a fixed rolling timetable with the same lower SAC controller, including a 360\,s reference and the best fixed headway from $\{300,330,360,390,420\}$\,s.
    \item Independent assembly: a fixed-timetable lower controller is trained first, frozen, and then paired with a trained timetable upper.
    \item Classical and RL holding baselines: Daganzo-style holding \citep{daganzo2009headway}, Xuan-style two-headway holding \citep{xuan2011dynamic}, and a same-simulator advantage-based auxiliary-reward RL proxy motivated by hierarchical RL and recent RL/MARL holding studies \citep{li2019hierarchical,singh2024piper,wang2021asynchronous,wang2023robust,yu2024hierarchical,xu2025systematic,li2026graphaware}. The RL proxy is not claimed as an exact reproduction of the original papers.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Baseline implementation and tuning.}
The Daganzo-style and Xuan-style controllers are same-simulator holding proxies with a fixed 360\,s timetable and holding clipped to 0--60\,s. The RL proxy shares the simulator, state and action representations, training budget, and lower policy architecture with TransitDuet, but uses a reachability-gated auxiliary upper-advantage signal. Fixed equations, parameter settings, and the test-set separation protocol are provided in \Cref{app:baseline_details}.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
In addition to average out-of-vehicle wait, headway CV, fleet overshoot, and the composite objective, we report the metrics required for a bus-holding and timetable-optimization study: average holding time, onboard passenger time, total passenger time, planned dispatch headway, actual dispatch headway, planned shift relative to the baseline timetable, and dispatch lateness. Timetable variability is reported as the within-episode standard deviation of planned dispatch headways, in seconds, together with its relationship to both station-level and dispatch-level realized headway CV.

\paragraph{Training and held-out evaluation.}
Each learned method is trained for 300 episodes per seed with ten seeds: 42, 123, 456, 789, 1001, 1002, 1003, 1004, 1005, and 1006. The final checkpoint, episode 299, is pre-specified by this training budget and is not selected from test outcomes. For every seed, we evaluate that checkpoint on 50 independent stochastic held-out episodes using a seed-specific fleet schedule. No held-out episode is used for checkpoint selection, parameter tuning, or timetable selection.
The main comparison reports the mean and sample standard deviation across the ten seed-level test means. This separates training from final evaluation while retaining the holding, passenger-time, and timetable-feasibility diagnostics.

TransitDuet achieves the lowest mean composite cost (1.431) in this comparison: 2.4\% below the target-headway-only variant (1.467), 4.1\% below the advantage-based RL proxy (1.492), and 6.2\% below the fixed-timetable SAC baseline with a 300\,s timetable (1.525). The two classical holding rules apply substantially longer holding (45--48\,s) yet yield higher waiting time and total passenger time than TransitDuet.

The planned-shift and lateness columns audit execution of the rolling timetable. Planned launch times are recursively anchored to the preceding actual launch time, so a fixed-headway policy can also accumulate non-zero shift from the baseline timetable. Consequently, planned shift SD is descriptive rather than direct evidence that the adaptive planner changes its action; that isolation is provided by the target-headway-only and fixed-timetable ablations. Dispatch lateness quantifies the delay between planned and actual dispatch caused by vehicle availability or fleet limits. The independent-assembly result indicates that the framework should be described as jointly trainable rather than as proving that joint training is always superior.

\subsection{Timetable variability and realized regularity}
\label{sec:mechanism}

For each final-window training episode, we compute the within-episode standard deviation of planned dispatch headways, in seconds, and two realized regularity measures: the station-level headway CV used in the main comparison and the CV of actual dispatch headways. This training-window diagnostic does not assume that greater planned variability is intrinsically beneficial. Instead, it distinguishes whether planner variation is reflected in actual dispatches from whether it has a monotonic relation with end-to-end station-level regularity, which is also affected by demand, traffic, and holding. The controlled ablations, rather than the correlations alone, identify the value of dynamic timetable decisions.

This distinction avoids treating a dispatch-time diagnostic as evidence that greater planner variability alone improves station-level regularity.
